# Visual cues for the four land-cover classes

Start with the full primary RGB. Anchor the decision at the marked location or inside the intended land unit. Identify the patch to which it belongs, then compare that patch with neighbouring cover under similar light. Use native detail for unresolved crown boundaries or texture.

## 2 · Cashew orchards

At crown scale, trace the visible foliage boundary of several nearby trees. Look for broad, blunt-rounded crowns with gently bulging edges or repeating soft lobes in a closed canopy. Separate the foliage from its cast shadow and the bare ground around it. Small dark dots and a fine-grained canopy can look round at map scale even when individual crown outlines are unresolved. In a sparse orchard, compare individual crowns and the gaps between them. A point in a normal inter-crown gap can still belong to the orchard. In a field with isolated retained trees, look for the continuity of the cultivated surface through the point.

At patch scale, follow the crown surface across the clearing edge, gaps and changes in shade. The orchard can have irregular spacing, interlocking crowns and taller intermingled trees. In that case look for a recurring rounded texture among several of its smaller crowns within the patch, sometimes connected to a more open part of the same unit. Compare crowns on both sides of a track or candidate boundary. Along mixed orchard edges in CIV and GHA, check both the outer stand and the interior. Brighter or warmer green can reinforce the crown pattern under similar lighting. Local colour also varies with season, shadow and image processing.

For a difficult woody patch, compare an orchard reference of the same form with the closest woody lookalike. CIV `0013` shows broad crown lobes. CIV `0008` and the Ghana references include fine dark crowns in regular rows and granular closed stands. Rows, regular gaps, a field edge, and their persistence over time describe planting geometry. Confirm Cashew with a distinguishable crown pattern or a clear local seasonal contrast tied to the same crowns. Another date can reveal those features and check for cover change at the same location. Check local cloud, haze and shadows first. A single clear dry-season view can establish Cashew when the features are distinct. Use 10 when crown identity remains unresolved.

## 1 · Mix tree, wetland and water

Look for open scrub and savanna, mixed woodland, other tree crops such as cocoa and rubber, and drainage or wetland vegetation. Follow recurring irregular heights, gaps, and shadow patterns within the patch. Confirm that a suspected woodland corridor continues along the actual drainage. Managed non-Cashew stands can be bright, round-crowned and regularly spaced. Compare whether their crowns form a fine, even row texture or show a coarser, variably layered surface alongside Cashew examples. Keep both interiors distinct where these covers meet. Water typically follows a continuous surface with banks and wetland vegetation along low areas.

## 0 · Cropland and other open land

Check the ground pattern at the point. Narrow planting lines, connected furrows, field-edge geometry and uniform patches of bare or harvested soil support a cultivated field. A green crop can form an even striped surface. A rounded tree within it can be incidental. Inspect whether broad crowns recur in a separate orchard parcel. Open savanna and fallow can look similar from far away. Trace natural woody clumps or surface preparation across the patch.

## 3 · Built-up land

Look for roofs, repeated hard corners, directional shadows, yards, compounds and branching access lanes. Use native detail around small settlements. The compound can include bare yard area between structures, so follow the compound edge. Open bare ground and broad roads should be judged with nearby structures and surface pattern.

## Source points and edges

Place SAM2 prompts inside the recognizable interiors on the full registered image. Preserve separable orchards near woodland and use 10 where crowns or land-unit identity remain mixed. Judge each unit before marking its boundary.
